1 · Quick start

  1. Click anywhere once so the browser allows alarm sound. The top bar should show 🔔 Sound on.
  2. The session opens lined out at design: 218.75 m³/h of light crude (33 000 barrels a day), heater outlet 388 °C, all five products on specification.
  3. Open P&ID to see the plant sheet by sheet, then DCS console to operate it. The display bar has the nine graphics 1–9 of the unit.
  4. Go to Exercises, pick Training, Think EQUIPPED or Procedures, and load one. A blue banner gives the situation; Acknowledge on the banner hides it.
  5. Click any instrument bubble, pump or switch on a graphic to open its faceplate on the right; change mode, set point or output there.
  6. Crude units are slow: use Speed 5×–20× and Freeze while you think. A cold start-up takes several simulated hours.
  7. When finished, open Debrief and grading for your score, then Data export to download CSV files for your report.

6 · Trends and alarms

Trends hold up to four hours at one point per second; choose the group, window (5–60 min) and pen colours. Selected tag shows PV, SP and OUT of the chosen controller. The alarm summary lists the newest unacknowledged alarm first; click a row to acknowledge and open that tag. Event alarms: EMS trip, low-flow trips bypassed, oil under-carry, relief valves lifting, boot overflow, wash zone dry, draw tray dry and high tube skin.

7 · Heater EMS

States IDLE → PURGING → PURGED → RUN. Purge needs both fuel EMS valves closed, FIC-106 and PIC-112 in MAN at 0 %, both passes above 40 m³/h (or HS-105A bypass) and no snuffing steam. Light on fuel gas only with FIC-106 in MAN at 3–15 %; a 10 s trial must prove flame. Fuel oil is admitted separately once PDC-111 shows atomising steam. Trips close both fuel valves and put TIC-101, FIC-106 and PIC-112 in MAN at 0 %. HS-105B is the emergency shutdown pushbutton.

8 · Field

Start and stop pumps (a tripped motor will not restart while its fault is active: use the spare), open and close block valves, set the steam bypass globe valves and the boot drain in %, and put snuffing steam into the firebox. Local gauges read the process directly and are the independent check when a transmitter is suspect. Laboratory samples report product qualities and salt in the desalted crude after 2 simulated minutes.

9 · Exercises

Training: 29 single-fault exercises starting from design. Think EQUIPPED: 12 cases with only a situation (the key is hidden in trainee mode). Procedures: cold start-up, normal shutdown, emergency shutdown, P-9A recovery and a 30-minute optimisation; steps tick automatically, "!" marks a step done out of order, some steps need Mark done. What-if: seven sets (tower bottom, top, tower, diesel stripper, AGO stripper, reflux drum, analyzers); predict each variable, then the model runs a hidden copy for 30 minutes. Trend matching, scrambles and the DCS reading sheet are graded into the Debrief.

10 · Instructor station

Inject any of the 38 faults (switch numbers follow the legacy console where one exists) with severity and ramp; clear them again. Load the design, cold or heavy-crude initial condition, save snapshots, backtrack every 1–5 minutes, change the purge time, switch noise, send written orders, and run the model check (the same direction cases used to validate the model offline).

11 · Debrief and export

The debrief shows the continuous score with each deduction, performance figures, exercise grades, fault recovery times and the event log (filter and copy). Data export writes CSV files (UTF-8) of the run data, a design-versus-now snapshot and the event log. On GitHub Pages the file downloads normally; in claude.ai the download prompt appears. Nothing is kept after the page is closed: export before you leave.

The process · atmospheric distillation of crude oil

1 · Principles of the process

What the unit does. Crude oil is a mixture of thousands of hydrocarbons boiling from below 0 °C to above 700 °C. The atmospheric crude unit separates it by boiling range into light ends (off-gas), naphtha, diesel, atmospheric gas oil (AGO) and atmospheric residue. No chemistry is intended: it is a physical separation by partial vaporisation and fractionation, run at just above atmospheric pressure. The residue goes on to the vacuum unit, which recovers the heavier gas oils without heating them above their cracking temperature.

  • The true boiling point (TBP) curve. The crude is described by the volume fraction that boils below each temperature. For the design light crude about 24 % boils below 193 °C (naphtha and lighter), 47 % below 343 °C and 52 % below 367 °C. The cut point between two products is the TBP temperature where one product ends and the next begins. The product yields follow directly from the cut points, so a yield change is a cut-point change.
  • Flash zone vaporisation. The heater raises the crude to about 388 °C. It enters the tower through the transfer line, where the pressure falls and part of it flashes (the temperature drops to about 371 °C). Everything that must leave as naphtha, diesel and AGO, plus a small overflash of about 3 % of the feed, has to be vapour here. At design the flash zone vaporises % of the crude. More heater outlet temperature, lower tower pressure or more stripping steam all raise this figure.
  • Why the outlet temperature is limited. Above about 370–400 °C the heaviest molecules start to crack thermally. They form gas and olefins, and coke builds up inside the heater tubes. Coke raises the tube-metal temperature and the pass pressure drop. The heater outlet (TIC-101, 388 °C) is therefore a compromise between lifting enough gas oil and keeping the coils clean. TSHH-1007 trips the heater at 410 °C.
  • Stripping steam. Steam injected below the flash zone and into the side strippers lowers the hydrocarbon partial pressure. Light material then boils off at a lower temperature. This recovers diesel from the residue and removes the light "tail" from diesel and AGO (it raises their 5 % and 10 % points and flash point). All the steam leaves overhead and condenses as sour water in the V-4 boot.
  • Fractionation needs reflux. The trays between two draws separate them only when liquid flows down against the rising vapour. That liquid, the internal reflux, comes from heat removal above: the top reflux (FIC-101) and the two pumparounds (MPA, BPA). The tower heat balance fixes how much heat must be removed. The top temperature controller (TIC-102) adjusts the top reflux to take out whatever the pumparounds leave. More pumparound duty means less top reflux, more heat recovered at a useful temperature, and less fractionation above the pumparound.
  • Heat integration. About % of the heat that takes the crude from storage to the heater outlet is recovered in 11 exchangers. The heat comes from the tower overhead, the products, the pumparounds and the vacuum-unit streams. The fired heater supplies the rest. Losing a pumparound or fouling an exchanger therefore shows up as more fuel burned and a lower heater inlet temperature (TI-1003).
  • Desalting. Crude carries brine. Its chlorides hydrolyse to HCl in the heater and corrode the overhead, and its salts foul exchangers and poison catalysts downstream. Wash water (about 2.7 vol % of the crude) is mixed in and the emulsion is broken in an electric field. The salt leaves with the brine. The desalters run at 1034 kPa g, well above the vapour pressure of water and light ends at 150 °C, so nothing boils in the vessel.
  • Pressure. The tower runs as low as the overhead system allows (48 kPa g at the top, 13.8 kPa g in the reflux drum). A higher pressure means less vaporisation at the same temperature (heavier residue, lighter products) and more fuel to reach the same lift.

3 · Process description

Crude charge and cold preheat (PID-001). Crude at about 53 °C is pumped from storage by P-1A/B at 218.75 m³/h (FIC-114, the feed master). Most of it passes through E-1, which condenses part of the tower overhead vapour. FCV-129 takes the flow that PIC-108 asks for, and FCV-130 bypasses about 10 m³/h round E-1. The crude then picks up heat from HVGO product in E-3, from diesel product in E-2 and from AGO product in E-14. It reaches the desalters at about 155 °C (TI-180).

Desalting (PID-002). FIC-132 splits the crude between two parallel desalters, V-3 and S-1. Wash water (FIC-103, FIC-133, 2.9 m³/h each, pumped by P-11A/B and warmed in E-13/E-19) is injected ahead of each vessel. The brine settles to an interface held at 15 % by LIC-103 and LIC-161 and leaves for treatment. PIC-108 holds the desalter outlet at 1034 kPa g by setting how much crude FCV-129 admits to the train. BV-102 bypasses both desalters in an emergency.

Hot preheat (PID-003). The desalted crude (about 149 °C) is boosted by P-2A/B to about 1940 kPa g and heated further:

  • E-15: HVGO reflux from the vacuum unit.
  • E-4: the mid pumparound (MPA).
  • E-5, then E-7: the bottom pumparound (BPA). The BPA goes through E-7 first, then E-5.
  • E-6: the vacuum-unit HVGO pumparound.
  • E-8 // E-18: vacuum residue. FIC-136 splits the crude over the pair and FIC-138 splits the residue.

The crude reaches the heater at about 276 °C (TI-1003).

Heater H-1 (PID-004). FIC-105A/B divide the crude equally between two radiant passes. FIC-114 is their master, so the passes follow the feed. The crude leaves at 388 °C (TIC-101) with part of it already vaporised. Fuel oil is the base load (PIC-112 at 689 kPa g, atomised with steam by PDC-111). Fuel gas (FIC-106) is the swing fuel chosen by HS-101 that TIC-101 moves. The heater EMS (UY-105) closes the fuel shutdown valves on these trips:

  • low pass flow (FSL-105A/B, 40 m³/h)
  • high-high coil outlet (TSHH-1007, 410 °C)
  • flame failure
  • manual ESD (HS-105B)

Crude tower V-1 (PID-005). The partly vaporised feed enters the flash zone below tray 4 at about 371 °C and 76 kPa g. The liquid falls to the bottom, where 3810 kg/h of stripping steam (FIC-109) recovers light material. The atmospheric residue leaves through P-3A/B to the vacuum unit (FIC-1102 in cascade from the bottoms level LIC-107). The vapour rises through 30 trays:

  • AGO is drawn from tray 12 (HCV-102).
  • The bottom pumparound is drawn from tray 9 and returns to tray 11.
  • The mid pumparound is drawn from tray 17 and returns to tray 19.
  • Diesel is drawn from tray 20 (HCV-101).
  • Top reflux returns to tray 30.

TIC-102 holds the top at 136 °C by setting the reflux (FIC-101).

Pumparounds (PID-006). P-9A/B circulate 87.5 m³/h of MPA through E-4 (FIC-104). P-10 circulates 99.6 m³/h of BPA through E-7 and E-5 (FIC-108). Together they remove about 7.9 MW from the middle of the tower and return it to the crude.

Side strippers (PID-007). Diesel and AGO flow by gravity into the strippers V-2A and V-2B. There, steam (FIC-111, FIC-112) strips the light ends back to the tower (trays 22 and 14). The stripper levels (LIC-108, LIC-109) set the product rates:

  • FIC-102: diesel by P-8A/B, cooled in E-2, E-11 and E-12.
  • FIC-113: AGO by P-7A/B, cooled in E-14.

On-line analyzers read the diesel 5 % and 90 % points (AI-103, AI-102) and the AGO 10 % point (AI-104).

Overhead (PID-008). The overhead vapour, naphtha plus all the steam, is condensed in E-1 (against crude), the air cooler E-9 (fan pitch HIC-100) and the water-cooled trim E-10. It is collected in the reflux drum V-4 at about 46 °C. The streams leave V-4 as follows:

  • Reflux: P-5A/B.
  • Unstabilised naphtha to the debutanizer: P-6A/B, FIC-100 in cascade from the drum level LIC-101. AI-100 and AI-101 read its IBP and end point.
  • Sour water from the boot: P-4 under LIC-102.
  • Uncondensed light ends: off-gas to the compressor.

PIC-102 holds the drum at 13.8 kPa g with a split-range scheme: natural gas in, then compressor spillback, then flare.

Utilities and safety. The unit uses:

  • stripping and atomising steam
  • fuel gas and fuel oil
  • cooling water for E-10 and E-12
  • electricity for pumps and fans
  • natural gas for drum pressure
  • the flare

The main hazards and their protections:

  • Loss of flow through a heater pass while firing: the coil overheats and cokes. The low-flow trip protects it.
  • A heater tube leak means a firebox fire: ESD, snuffing steam, isolate the passes.
  • Water reaching a hot tower flashes violently and damages trays. Check the boot level and never admit wet crude to a hot tower.
  • Overpressure of the desalters (PSV-108) and the drum (PSV-102).

1 · Purpose

This workbook trains you to operate, monitor and troubleshoot the atmospheric section of a crude distillation unit: preheat train, desalters, two-pass fired heater, 30-tray crude tower with two pumparounds, diesel and AGO side strippers, and the overhead system. By the end you should be able to:

  1. Read the P&ID sheets and the nine DCS graphics, and state the normal value and alarm limits of every key tag.
  2. Explain how heat input, pressure, stripping steam, reflux, pumparounds and draw rates set the yield and quality of each product, and predict the direction every key variable moves after a change.
  3. Start the unit from cold through the heater EMS, line it out at design and bring every product on specification.
  4. Recognise equipment, instrument and process faults from alarms, trends and local checks, make the unit safe, and find the root cause with a systematic six-stage method.
  5. Improve the operation (yield, energy, flaring) and support your case with exported data.

2 · The unit

Crude from storage is pumped by P-1A/B through the overhead exchanger E-1 (FIC-129, with bypass FIC-130), E-3 (HVGO product), E-2 (diesel product) and E-14 (AGO product) to the two parallel desalters V-3 and S-1 (FIC-132 split, wash water by P-11A/B, brine on interface control). The booster pumps P-2A/B send it on through E-15 (HVGO reflux), E-4 (mid pumparound), E-5 and E-7 (bottom pumparound), E-6 (HVGO from the vacuum tower) and the parallel vacuum-residue exchangers E-8/E-18 to the two passes of heater H-1. Partly vaporised crude enters the flash zone of V-1 below tray 5. Four stripping trays below the flash zone use steam FIC-109. Diesel is drawn from tray 20 and AGO from tray 12 to their steam strippers V-2A and V-2B; the mid pumparound runs from tray 17 back to tray 19, the bottom pumparound from tray 9 back to tray 11. Overhead vapour is condensed in E-1, the fin-fan E-9 and the trim cooler E-10 into reflux drum V-4, which separates off-gas (to the compressor), naphtha (reflux and product) and sour water (boot, P-4). Residue goes to the vacuum unit.

Values are the model's lined-out design point. Rebasing and assumptions: SI units throughout; the vacuum-unit hot streams, the crude TBP curves, the heater efficiency (about 55 % of the fired heat reaches the crude) and a few preheat temperatures were set by balance where the source gave none.

3 · Control scheme

Key ideas: the crude rate is set by FIC-114 through the heater pass flows, and the desalter pressure PIC-108 pulls the matching crude through E-1. The heater outlet TIC-101 moves only the swing fuel. The top temperature sets how much naphtha goes overhead; the reflux is simply what the heat balance needs to hold it, so anything that removes less heat lower down (a pumparound, a fouled exchanger) shows up as more reflux. The draw valves set the diesel and AGO yields; everything not drawn off goes to the residue.

4 · Safety systems

Heater EMS: IDLE → PURGING → PURGED → RUN. Any trip closes the fuel gas and fuel oil EMS valves and puts the firing controllers in MAN at zero; the first cause is latched. Reset, purge and relight on gas every time.

5 · Operating rules

  1. Never let water reach a hot tower. During start-up hold the heater outlet at 121 °C until water stops arriving in the V-4 boot, and never let the boot overflow.
  2. Keep the desalters liquid-full and above the bubble point (PIC-108 about 1034 kPa). Watch the interface: too low sends oil to water treatment, too high sends water with the crude.
  3. Protect the heater coils: both passes must flow before firing; a pass that loses flow overheats in seconds. Do not bypass the low-flow trips (HS-105A) except as the procedure says.
  4. Change one thing at a time and let the tower settle (10–20 minutes) before judging the result.
  5. Draw rate moves the heavy end, stripping steam moves the light end. Adjust the product and its neighbour together.
  6. Do not over-draw: if the draw trays run dry the overflash is lost and the wash zone cokes.
  7. Keep the drum pressure just above atmospheric (13.8 kPa g) and flaring to a minimum.
  8. Check two instruments before you believe one: TIC-101 against TI-1007A/B, FIC-114 against the pass flows and the local FG-114, PIC-108 against PG-108, FIC-101 against the local reflux gauge, LIC-107 against the sight glass.
